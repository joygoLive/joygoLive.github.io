Design

Three decisions that shaped it

Food-and-music pairing has been tried several times. The earlier attempts either paired photos with songs and were absorbed by larger social apps, relied on curators and did not scale, or treated pairing as a formula and stopped being fun.

The unit is dish × song × momentA dish alone converges on a single right answer, which is a recipe site. Tagging each pairing with when and with whom it was eaten turns the difference between moments into the thing people compare.
Posted by the people eating, not curatedOnly someone who ate it can say what fits. A pairing is a moment, a dish and a song, with an optional photo. No name, phone number or email is collected; the server issues each person a nickname built from something to eat.
Humor is a reaction, not a genreThree reactions: a perfect fit, a forced fit, and one that does not fit but is funny. The third keeps absurd pairings in the ranking instead of burying them.
Posting

Four fields, one of them optional

Posting has to be quicker than deciding not to. The dish field offers dishes already posted before anything new, so the same dish does not split into several spellings.

A photo, if you want oneRe-encoded in the browser to at most 1440 px on the long edge before upload, which also strips the location stored in the photo. The server identifies the format from the file's first bytes, not its name.
The momentOne of eight: eating alone, with friends, team dinner, date, late night, hangover, rainy day, delivery. It is the axis a dish page compares along.
The dishSpacing and punctuation are ignored when dishes are compared, so every spelling of the same dish lands on the same page.
One song and one linePicked from a public music catalog with a 30-second preview, plus a comment of up to 140 characters. How the song is found and checked is described in the next section.

Every pairing becomes a share card. A 1080 × 1920 portrait image sized for stories, and a link that shows the photo and song in chat previews before anyone opens it.

Song catalog

One fixed id per song

Counting which song fits a dish requires everyone who picks the same recording to land on the same key. A free-text title cannot guarantee that; a catalog id can.

Apple's public catalog, US storefrontThe iTunes Search API needs no key and gives each track a fixed id. The Korean storefront returns no songs at all, so the US storefront is used; Korean songs keep their Hangul titles, with artist names romanized.
Search runs in the visitor's browserCalled from Cloudflare, the catalog sees a shared egress address used by many services and throttles it per minute, answering with an empty list rather than an error. Called from each browser, every visitor has a separate quota. A server-side search remains as the fallback and caches only non-empty results.
US and Japan in parallelJapanese songs and recordings by Japanese performers are often missing from the US store, which returns a similar but different track instead. Both stores are queried at once: US results first, Japanese results only where they do not overlap. A query with no hits is retried word by word.
Re-lookup on submitThe server looks the track up again by id and caches found tracks for a month. If that lookup is throttled, the track sent by the page is accepted only when its id matches and its artwork, preview and link URLs point to the catalog's own hosts.

Listening links: YouTube search and Apple Music. Most listeners in Korea use YouTube, so each pairing offers a YouTube search next to the catalog link and the 30-second preview.

Moderation

Content posted by guests needs a floor

Most of this content happens over drinks. The rules are few and applied the same way every time, so moderation is a queue rather than a judgment call per post.

Adults only, confirmed onceBefore the first post: 19 or older and the house rules. Reading needs neither.
Three reports hide a postFrom three different people. The post stays hidden until an operator keeps it, hides it, or hides it and restricts the author. Each report and each automatic hide notifies the operator immediately.
Reactions reach you, at most hourlyWeb push, one notification per pairing per hour, carrying the counts accumulated since the last one. A conditional update on the pairing's last-sent time acts as the lock, so simultaneous reactions cannot each send one.
Counts recomputed on every reactionThe three reaction counts are recounted from the reaction table each time. Incrementing and decrementing drifts under concurrent requests, and a drifted count never corrects itself.
Deleting keeps the rowDeleting your own pairing takes it down and removes the photo from storage, but keeps the record, so report history and reaction totals stay consistent.
Limits

What this does not claim

Listing what is out of scope is more useful than listing features, because most of it was left out on purpose.

No music is servedA 30-second preview from the catalog and links out to listen. No audio file is stored or streamed from here.
Not a review siteNo ratings, prices or opening hours. Where a dish was eaten is not recorded.
One browser, one identityIdentity is a one-year cookie in one browser, with no transfer between devices. Safari clears it after seven days without a visit; the next visit starts a new nickname, and earlier posts stay up.
Eight moments, fixedChosen to cover most meals without overlapping. They change only in code, when the share per moment shows one crowding out the rest.
Catalog coverage has gapsSome Korean songs appear under English titles or romanized artist names. A song known in Korea by a translated title is found only by its original title.
iPhone alerts need the home screenSafari does not deliver web push to a browser tab. The app suggests adding it to the home screen right after the first pairing and on the My YumJam page.
