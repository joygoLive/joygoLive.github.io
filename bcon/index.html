BCON · Broadcast Commute Observation Networks

The wireless around you, checked and acted on.

The summary names what is wrong with the networks in range — no encryption, one name answering from several radios, protections that were never switched on — and then what to do about each one. A little of it is a setting on a router. Most of it is a habit on the phone in your pocket.

What it checks What to do about it

It receives, and nothing else. Beacons are already broadcast in the clear; BCON listens to that and stops there. It never connects, never authenticates, never cracks, and never names an individual access point on screen.

Premise

Almost none of it is yours to fix

Reports on how insecure wireless is in general already exist, and a national average changes nothing you do tomorrow. Worse, nearly every device in range belongs to somebody else, so "harden it" is not on the table. What is left is knowing which ones are dangerous and behaving accordingly — which only works if the check ends in an instruction.

A place, not a populationAn aggregate needs a large sample before it means anything. Something actually picked up in one spot is a fact at n = 1, and it stays worth re-checking as long as you keep going there.
A finding with a next stepA score on its own is a number to feel bad about. Every item here carries the thing to change: a toggle, a setting, or a rule about what not to do on that network.
Repetition is the datasetSeeing the same places repeatedly is usually a limit on coverage. Here it is the point: the same spot read again and again becomes a record of how it changed.
Checks

Five things that put you at risk

Each carries a weight, because they are not equally dangerous. An open network you might join by accident is worth four times a stale cipher on equipment you were never going to touch. The weights add up to one score per place, and the score always shows its parts.

Open network40

No encryption at all. The real hazard is not the network but the phone, which may have been told to join something like it before and will do so again without asking.

Evil twin20

One familiar name answering from several different radios. Mesh systems and phone hotspots do this legitimately, so it is reported as a candidate to look at, never as an accusation.

No management-frame protection15

Without PMF, anyone nearby can knock a device off the network — and a forced reconnection is exactly the moment an impostor wants. Optional PMF is flagged separately from absent.

WPS enabled15

The convenience pairing path, and a way around the passphrase that the passphrase cannot defend against. Usually on because nobody turned it off.

Weak cipher10

WEP, original WPA, or TKIP still in the mix. Lowest weight because it usually appears on equipment old enough that nobody was going to connect to it anyway.

  • The unit is a place, not a network. Readings are grouped into cells about 111 m across and sorted by how long you stayed, because the place you linger in is the place you are exposed in. A corridor you pass through in ninety seconds is not the same risk as the desk you sit at all day.
  • Guesswork is labelled as guesswork. Default network names, vendor from the address prefix, and device type from the name are all inference. They are printed as circumstantial, and a randomised address returns no vendor at all rather than a wrong one.
Actions

What to change, item by item

Two kinds of instruction come out of a reading. On equipment you administer there is usually a setting to change. On everything else — which is most of it — the fix lives on your own device, in what it is allowed to join automatically.

  • Open network → stop joining them by reflex. Turn off automatic connection to open networks. If one has to be used, treat everything on it as readable and put a VPN in front. Some are opened as bait precisely because phones join them unprompted.
  • Evil twin candidate → connect by hand, not by memory. If a name you know from home or the office appears somewhere it should not, or answers from several radios at once, turn off auto-join for that name and pick the network deliberately. Most candidates are mesh or a hotspot, which is why they are candidates and not accusations.
  • WPS → switch it off where you can, distrust it where you cannot. On your own router it is one setting, and turning it off costs you nothing but the pairing button. On someone else's, treat that network as one your passphrase does not protect, because theirs does not protect theirs.
  • No PMF → move to WPA3 if the hardware allows. WPA3 makes management-frame protection mandatory, which is the single change that removes this item. Where the equipment cannot do it, the instruction is behavioural: do not do anything on that network you would mind having interrupted and redirected.
  • Default name or old cipher → assume the rest is default too. A network still carrying its factory name is circumstantial evidence that the admin password was never changed either. On your own equipment, change both and move off TKIP and WEP to AES. On other people's, read it as a sign of how the box is maintained.

  • Counting has to be honest before anything else is. Most of what answers a scan is too weak to connect to, and the same router answers twice on two bands. Count all of it and the risk score falls as the neighbourhood gets noisier — which is backwards. The score above is computed only over what is actually joinable.
  • Management-frame protection is the single biggest item, everywhere so far. It is what stops someone knocking you off the network to make you reconnect somewhere else, and WPA3 is what makes it mandatory. So far the WPA3 figure above has stayed at zero.
  • This is why the answer is behavioural. Practically every device in a reading belongs to somebody else. No amount of tightening your own settings changes their equipment, so the useful output is a rule about what your phone is allowed to join.
Limits

What this does not claim

  • Nobody else is a target. The output is about exposure, not about fault. There is no ranking of networks, no naming of an owner, and no suggestion that a particular access point is to blame. What appears on screen is deliberately not enough to go and find one.
  • Inference is not identification. Device type, vendor and "default name" are read off broadcast text and address prefixes. They are frequently right and sometimes wrong, and are labelled so you can tell which kind of claim you are looking at.
  • One block is one block. A reading describes the place it was taken in and nothing beyond it. It is not a survey of a city, a carrier or a manufacturer, and a figure from it should never be quoted as one.
  • None of the technique is new. Reading what is broadcast has been possible for twenty years, and mapping projects have covered the world with it. What is different here is the question asked of it and the instruction that comes out — which is a thinner advantage than a technical one.
  • Readings stay with whoever took them. Anything derived from where a person has been carries obligations of its own, so none of it is distributed, sold, or handed to a third party. That is a property of the design, not a policy that could be relaxed later.
