BCON · Broadcast · Commute · Observation · Networks

The air on your usual routes, read for your own risk.

A spare phone rides along on the routes you already take and reads what the air broadcasts. The question it answers is not whether somebody else is vulnerable. It is whether you are exposed, at the places where you actually spend your hours.

What it reads What it will not claim

It receives, and nothing else. Beacons are already broadcast in the clear; BCON listens to that and stops there. It never connects, never authenticates, never cracks, and never names an individual access point on screen.

An MVP by joygoLive. Private while it is still collecting.
Premise

Self-protection, not a survey

Reports on how insecure wireless is in general already exist, and knowing the national average changes nothing you do tomorrow. The one thing nobody measures is the route you personally walk every day. That is the only sample where the number is about you.

The audience is youAn aggregate needs a large sample before it means anything. A specific event on your own route means something at n = 1, and it stays worth re-checking because the route is still yours.
Zero added effortNothing asks you to go somewhere. The phone reads in the background along the way you were going anyway — closer to a dashcam than to a survey.
Repetition is the datasetGoing to the same places is usually a limit on coverage. Here it is the point: the same corridor measured again and again becomes a record of how it changed.
Signals

Five things that put you at risk

Each carries a weight, because they are not equally dangerous. An open network you might join by accident is worth four times a stale cipher on a network you never touch. The weights add up to one score per place, and the score always shows its parts.

Open network40

No encryption at all. The real hazard is not the network but your phone, which may have been told to join something like it before and will do so again without asking.

Evil twin20

One familiar name answering from several different radios. Mesh systems do this legitimately, so it is reported as a candidate to look at, never as an accusation.

No management-frame protection15

Without PMF, anyone nearby can knock you off the network — and a forced reconnection is exactly the moment an impostor wants. Optional PMF is flagged separately from absent.

WPS enabled15

The convenience pairing path, and a way around the passphrase that the passphrase cannot defend against. Usually on because nobody turned it off.

Weak cipher10

WEP, original WPA, or TKIP still in the mix. Lowest weight because it usually appears on equipment old enough that you were not going to connect to it anyway.

  • The unit is a place, not a network. Observations are grouped into cells about 111 m across and sorted by how long you stayed, because the place you linger in is the place you are exposed in. A corridor you pass through in ninety seconds is not the same risk as the desk you sit at all day.
  • Guesswork is labelled as guesswork. Default network names, vendor from the address prefix, and device type from the name are all inference. They are printed as circumstantial, and a randomised address returns no vendor at all rather than a wrong one.
Pipeline

Three stages, each with a line it does not cross

Collection has to survive a phone in a pocket with no cable attached, and the summary has to be useful without holding a register of who owns what.

01
Collect

A logger on the spare phone asks the system for a scan and writes what comes back, reusing the location fix that is already running. It is detached from the session that started it, so unplugging the cable and walking out does not stop it.

Line: receive only. No association, no authentication, no probing of anything.
02
Accumulate

The phone pushes lines to a server at home over a tunnel, so it keeps reporting from a mobile connection rather than waiting until it is back on the desk. The raw log is appended to and never rewritten.

Line: the server binds to loopback. Only the tunnel reaches it, and the dashboard is behind a password.
03
Summarise

One score per place with its parts shown, plus every access point folded to a single row carrying only what matters to you: how it is secured, what kind of device it looks like, and what is unusual about it.

Line: hardware addresses are used to detect twins and are not shown. The screen carries a fragment, never the whole.
First run

Twenty minutes, one place

The first collection ran at home and nowhere else. It is a shakedown of the pipeline, not a finding about anywhere — but the shape of what came back is already the point of the whole thing.

One location, one twenty-minute window, one device. Percentages are of the 33 named access points. Read it as a working pipeline, not as a statement about anywhere.

  • One item carried half the score. Missing management-frame protection contributed 14.5 of 29.0. A single score would have hidden that; showing the parts makes the one thing worth acting on obvious immediately.
  • The exposure is other people's equipment. Almost none of this is yours to fix, which is exactly why the answer is "know it and behave accordingly" rather than "harden it".
  • Detached collection was the part that had to be proven. With the cable pulled and the debugging session killed, the log kept growing. Collection that only works while tethered to a desk would measure the one place that needs it least.
Limits

What this does not claim

  • Nobody else is a target. The output is about your exposure. There is no ranking of networks, no naming of an owner, and no notion that a particular access point is at fault. What is on screen is deliberately not enough to go and find one.
  • Inference is not identification. Device type, vendor and "default name" are read off broadcast text and address prefixes. They are frequently right and sometimes wrong, and are labelled so you can tell which claim you are looking at.
  • The sample is one person's routes. That is the design, not a shortfall — but it means nothing here generalises. It is not a survey of a city, a carrier, or a manufacturer, and it should never be quoted as one.
  • None of the technique is new. A phone that scans and logs is twenty years old, and mapping projects have covered the world with it. What is different is only the question being asked of the data, and that is a thinner advantage than a technical one.
  • It is not public, and going public is a separate decision. The MVP collects privately. Publishing anything derived from location has its own rules to satisfy first, and that review has not been done — so it has not been published.
