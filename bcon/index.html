BCON · Broadcast Commute Observation Networks

The wireless around you, checked and acted on.

The summary names what is wrong with the networks in range — no encryption, one name answering from several radios, protections that were never switched on — and then what to do about each one. A little of it is a setting on a router. Most of it is a habit on the phone in your pocket.

What it checks What to do about it

It receives, and nothing else. Beacons are already broadcast in the clear; BCON listens to that and stops there. It never connects, never authenticates, never cracks, and never names an individual access point on screen.

Premise

Almost none of it is yours to fix

Reports on how insecure wireless is in general already exist, and a national average changes nothing you do tomorrow. Worse, nearly every device in range belongs to somebody else, so "harden it" is not on the table. What is left is knowing which ones are dangerous and behaving accordingly — which only works if the check ends in an instruction.

A place, not a populationAn aggregate needs a large sample before it means anything. Something actually picked up in one spot is a fact at n = 1, and it stays worth re-checking as long as you keep going there.
A finding with a next stepA score on its own is a number to feel bad about. Each reading comes back as things to do, ordered by how much risk each one removes — and grouped by the action, not the item, because three different findings often end in the same single move.
Repetition is the datasetSeeing the same places repeatedly is usually a limit on coverage. Here it is the point: the same spot read again and again becomes a record of how it changed.
Checks

Seven things that put you at risk

Each carries a weight, because they are not equally dangerous. An open network you might join by accident carries more than ten times the weight of a transition mode left on by the manufacturer. The weights add up to one score per place, and the score always shows its parts.

Open network35

No encryption at all. The real hazard is not the network but the phone, which may have been told to join something like it before and will do so again without asking.

Evil twin18

One familiar name answering from several different radios. Mesh systems and phone hotspots do this legitimately, so it is reported as a candidate to look at, never as an accusation.

No management-frame protection15

Without PMF, anyone nearby can knock a device off the network — and a forced reconnection is exactly the moment an impostor wants. Optional PMF is flagged separately from absent.

WPS enabled12

The convenience pairing path, and a way around the passphrase that the passphrase cannot defend against. Usually on because nobody turned it off.

Carrier roaming12

Several access points carrying one name so the phone hands itself over without asking. Convenient, and a record of where you went. The handover is also the easiest moment for an impostor to step in, because the phone is already expecting to move.

Weak cipher5

WEP, original WPA, or TKIP still in the mix. Lowest weight because it usually appears on equipment old enough that nobody was going to connect to it anyway.

Transition mode3

WPA3 switched on while WPA2 is left open for older devices — the manufacturer's default. An attacker simply chooses the WPA2 side, where none of WPA3's protections apply. Weighted lowest because it is still better than no WPA3 at all.

The unit is a place, not a networkA place is whatever you named it when you stood there — not a GPS cell. Indoors the coordinate freezes on the last fix, so three separate rooms measured in one evening all landed on the same square; the name you typed was the only thing that told them apart. Places are ordered by how long you stayed, because the place you linger in is the place you are exposed in.
Guesswork is labelled as guessworkDefault network names, vendor from the address prefix, and device type from the name are all inference. They are printed as circumstantial, and a randomised address returns no vendor at all rather than a wrong one.
Actions

What to do, in the order that helps most

Grouped by the action, not by the finding — open networks, evil twins and missing protection all end in the same first move, and listing them separately would say it three times. Ordered by how much of the score each action removes. These are suggestions, not instructions: nobody can change a stranger’s router, so the ones that need a settings change are written as "if any of these is yours".

① Turn off auto-join, choose by handOne move that closes both open networks and evil twins, which is why it is always first. An open network carries your traffic in the clear; when one name answers from several radios, the phone takes the strongest and cannot tell which is real. A VPN does not replace this — it protects what you send after you have joined, not the joining.
② Use a VPN in that spotWithout PMF anyone nearby can knock you off the network. The disconnection is not the problem; the reconnection is, and once you are on, encryption of the traffic is the only defence left. ① stops you joining, ② protects you after you have.
③ Drop auto-join for carrier hotspotsSeveral access points under one name means the phone hands itself over without asking. That handover is the easiest moment to step into, because the phone is already expecting to move — and the trail of where you went stays with that network.
④ Turn WPS off — if any of these is yoursThe PIN is validated in two halves, a design flaw that falls in hours, and once it falls changing the passphrase does not help — WPS lets them back in. On someone else's equipment, treat that network as one a passphrase does not protect.
⑤ Move off the old cipher — if any of these is yoursWEP and TKIP are effectively unprotected, and WPA2/WPA3 transition mode lets an attacker simply pick the weaker side. Moving to WPA3 makes PMF mandatory, which removes ② as well — and if the name is still the factory one, change the admin password with it.
Measured

Counting has to be honest before anything else isMost of what answers a scan is too weak to connect to, and the same router answers twice on two bands. Count all of it and the risk score falls as the neighbourhood gets noisier — which is backwards. Everything here counts only what is within connecting distance.
Management-frame protection is the single biggest item, everywhere so farIt is what stops someone knocking you off the network to make you reconnect somewhere else, and WPA3 is what makes it mandatory. The WPA3 figure above is that number.
This is why the answer is behaviouralPractically every device in a reading belongs to somebody else. No amount of tightening your own settings changes their equipment, so the useful output is a rule about what your phone is allowed to join.
Limits

What this does not claim

Put the limits anywhere but next to the reading, and a scan report reads as an accusation. Below is the range one reading can speak for.

Nobody else is a targetThe output is about exposure, not about fault. There is no ranking of networks, no naming of an owner, and no suggestion that a particular access point is to blame. What appears on screen is deliberately not enough to go and find one.
Inference is not identificationDevice type, vendor and "default name" are read off broadcast text and address prefixes. They are frequently right and sometimes wrong, and are labelled so you can tell which kind of claim you are looking at.
One block is one blockA reading describes the place it was taken in and nothing beyond it. It is not a survey of a city, a carrier or a manufacturer, and a figure from it should never be quoted as one.
None of the technique is newReading what is broadcast has been possible for twenty years, and mapping projects have covered the world with it. What is different here is the question asked of it and the instruction that comes out — which is a thinner advantage than a technical one.
Readings stay with whoever took themAnything derived from where a person has been carries obligations of its own, so none of it is distributed, sold, or handed to a third party. That is a property of the design, not a policy that could be relaxed later.
